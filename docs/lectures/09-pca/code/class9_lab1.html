Introduction

In Labs 5 and 6 we classified breast tumors from 30 measurements. Nobody can look at a table with 30 columns and see what is in it. This lab asks two questions about the same data:

Can 30 correlated measurements be squeezed into a few numbers without losing much? And can those few numbers still tell a benign tumor from a malignant one?

The first question is principal component analysis (PCA). The second is principal components regression (PCR): fit the regression on the first few principal components instead of on the original features.

The plan:

  1. Look at the data. Thirty features, many of them close to copies of each other.
  2. Scaling. Why PCA on the raw data tells us nothing.
  3. PCA by hand. Eigenvalues, loadings and scores, checked against prcomp().
  4. How many components? The scree plot and its rules of thumb.
  5. Reading the components. What do the loadings say?
  6. A map of the tumors. Two numbers per tumor, coloured by diagnosis, and a check on whether the biggest component is the useful one.
  7. PCA as compression. Reconstructing the data from a few components.
  8. PCR. Logistic regression on the first \(M\) components, with \(M\) chosen by cross-validation.

A note on the tools

  • PCA has no outcome, so caret::train() does not apply. We use base R’s prcomp(). We also compute the eigendecomposition ourselves with eigen() and check that the two agree.
  • PCR is two steps, so we write the two steps. The pls package has a pcr() function for a numeric outcome, but our outcome is a diagnosis (benign or malignant). So we fit the principal components, then fit a logistic regression on them. The slides write PCR with a linear regression, \(Y = Z\theta + \epsilon\). For a binary outcome, the same idea gives \(\text{logit}\, P(Y = 1) = \theta_0 + Z\theta\).

The data: Wisconsin breast tumors

This is the same dataset as Labs 5 and 6: 569 tumors from fine-needle-aspirate images (Street, Wolberg and Mangasarian, 1993). Ten properties of the cell nuclei are measured (radius, texture, smoothness, and so on). For each property the data has its mean, its standard error, and its “worst” value in the image, giving 30 columns.

The diagnosis is not given to PCA. PCA is unsupervised. We keep the diagnosis on the side and use it only to check what PCA found.

Many features say the same thing

A tumor with a large radius also has a large perimeter and a large area. The correlation matrix shows how much of this there is. The 30 columns come in three blocks of 10 (means, standard errors, worst values), in the same order in each block.

21 pairs of features have a correlation above 0.9 in absolute value. The radius and the area of the same tumor have a correlation of 0.987. These columns carry much less than 30 separate pieces of information. That is what PCA exploits: if features move together, a few combinations of them can stand in for all of them.

Part 1 — Scaling comes first

The slides say PCA finds the direction of maximum variance. Variance depends on the units. Look at the variances of the features:

area_worst has a variance of about 324,000. Some features have a variance below 0.0001. Areas are measured in the hundreds, smoothness in hundredths. What does PCA do with the raw data?

The first component “explains” 98% of the variance. But look at its loadings: area_worst has weight 0.85, area_mean 0.52, and nothing else matters. PC1 is just the area of the tumor. It is a fact about the units, not about the tumors. The other 27 features have such small variances that PCA cannot see them.

We center and scale every feature to mean 0 and variance 1 first. Then all 30 features have the same say, and PCA looks at the correlations between them. In prcomp(), this is scale. = TRUE.

Now PC1 explains 44% of the variance, which is a more honest number. Rule: scale before PCA, unless the features share a unit and you have a reason to keep their natural scale.

Part 2 — PCA by hand

TipDo this on paper first

In class we found the loadings by maximizing variance. Here is the smallest case where you can do it all by hand. Take two standardized features with correlation \(\rho\). Their correlation matrix is \(R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\).

  1. Find the two eigenvalues of \(R\). (Solve \(\det(R - \lambda I) = 0\).) What is their sum? Why must it equal 2?
  2. Find the unit-length eigenvector for each eigenvalue. These are the loadings \((\phi_{11}, \phi_{21})\) and \((\phi_{12}, \phi_{22})\). Check that they are orthogonal and that \(\phi_{1m}^2 + \phi_{2m}^2 = 1\).
  3. What is the proportion of variance explained by PC1, as a function of \(\rho\)?
  4. What happens when \(\rho = 0\)? When \(\rho\) is close to 1? What would you conclude about PCA on features that are uncorrelated?

Then use your answer for radius_mean and area_mean, which have \(\rho = 0.987\), and compare with the code below.

The eigenvalues are \(1 + \rho\) and \(1 - \rho\), the loadings are \((1, 1)/\sqrt{2}\) and \((1, -1)/\sqrt{2}\), and PC1 explains \((1 + \rho)/2\) of the variance. PC1 is the average of the two standardized features (size), and PC2 is their difference (what is left over). When \(\rho\) is close to 1, the average carries nearly all of the information, and one number replaces two. When \(\rho = 0\), both eigenvalues are 1: there is no direction worth keeping.

Part 3 — How many components?

The proportion of variance explained (PVE) by component \(m\) is \(\lambda_m / \sum_j \lambda_j\). Here the denominator is 30. The scree plot shows the PVE for each component, and the cumulative PVE shows what is kept.

Three rules of thumb, and they do not agree:

  • Elbow. The scree plot bends around component 3 or 4. After that, each component adds little.
  • Cumulative PVE. 5 components keep 80% of the variance, 7 keep 90%, and 10 keep 95%. The slides suggest the smallest \(M\) that reaches 80%.
  • Variance above 1. A component with variance below 1 explains less than one original standardized feature. Six components pass this test.

So the 30 features can be summarized by roughly 5 to 10 numbers. There is no single right answer, because without an outcome there is nothing to cross-validate. When there is an outcome, as in PCR (Part 7), we can choose \(M\) by cross-validation.

Part 4 — Reading the components

A component is a recipe: a weight (loading) for each of the 30 standardized features. The three biggest:

  • PC1: overall abnormality. All 30 loadings have the same sign (after our flip, positive). A tumor with a high PC1 score is high on nearly every feature. The biggest weights are on concavity, concave points, compactness and the size features (perimeter, radius). The smallest are on texture and smoothness. PC1 is a weighted average that mostly measures the size and irregularity of the nuclei.
  • PC2: size against roughness. The size features (radius, area, perimeter) have positive loadings. The fractal dimension features (a measure of how rough the outline is) have the largest negative ones. A high PC2 means large nuclei with a smooth outline. A low PC2 means smaller nuclei with a rough outline. A contrast like this is a pattern in the data that no single feature shows.
  • PC3: variability within the image. The largest loadings are on the standard-error features (texture_se, smoothness_se, symmetry_se). This component is about how much the nuclei within one image vary, not about their typical values.

A warning. Names like “abnormality” or “roughness” are our reading of the loadings. PCA does not know what the features mean. Another analyst can name the same component differently, and a component can mix several things.

Part 5 — A map of the tumors

Two numbers per tumor can be drawn. We plot the first two scores and colour by diagnosis. PCA never saw the diagnosis.

The two diagnoses fall in different parts of the plot, mostly along PC1. A single rule, “PC1 above 0 means malignant”, is right for 92% of the tumors, with no labels used to build it. This is what the slides mean by “a simple visualization that shows a clear underlying pattern”. It also tells us that a predictive model has a good chance.

Is the biggest component the useful one?

PCA orders components by variance. It does not know the diagnosis, so there is no promise that the highest-variance direction is the one that separates the groups. Here we can check. For each component, the AUC of its score for telling malignant from benign (0.5 is no signal, 1 is perfect; we take max(auc, 1 - auc) because the sign is arbitrary):

PC1 has an AUC of 0.97. Every other component is at 0.62 or below. In these data, the direction of most variance is also the direction that separates the groups. That is good luck, and it is what makes PCR work in Part 7. In other data, the signal can sit in a component with little variance. Then PCR with a small \(M\) throws it away.

Part 6 — PCA as compression

The slides gave PCA a second description: the \(M\) components give the best \(M\)-dimensional approximation of the data. We build that approximation from the first \(M\) scores and loadings, \(\hat X = Z_{1:M}\, \Phi_{1:M}^\top\), and measure what is lost:

The two rows are the same. The share lost when we rebuild the data from \(M\) components is exactly \(1 -\) the cumulative PVE. So “keep the directions of maximum variance” and “make the reconstruction error as small as possible” are the same goal. With 10 components, we keep 95% of the information in the data, and we use a third of the columns.

Part 7 — Principal components regression

Now the outcome. We want to predict the diagnosis from the 30 features.

Why not use all 30?

As in Lab 6, we use the same split: 75% of the tumors for training, and 25% for testing.

R warns that the algorithm did not converge. The features are so correlated, and the classes so separable in 30 dimensions, that the coefficients grow without limit. The estimates have no meaning. This is the instability of OLS under collinearity that the slides described. Lab 6 fixed it with a penalty (the lasso). PCR fixes it by changing the predictors.

Step 1: PCA on the training set only

PCA uses only the features, not the outcome. Even so, we fit it on the training tumors, and then we project the test tumors with the training means, SDs and loadings. We do not refit on the test set: a new patient arrives one at a time, and our model must be able to score each of them alone.

Step 2: Choose \(M\) by cross-validation

For each \(M\) from 1 to 20, we fit a logistic regression on the first \(M\) scores. We use 10-fold cross-validation and measure two things: the deviance (a measure of fit that punishes confident mistakes, lower is better) and the accuracy.

To keep the code short, PCA is fitted once on the training set, and then the folds are used for the regression only. PCA never sees \(y\), so the leak is small.

The deviance has a clear U shape. It falls fast up to about 4 components, reaches its lowest value at \(M = 8\) (the red line), and then rises. Past about 15 components it explodes, because the model has started to separate the training data perfectly (the same problem as the 30-feature fit above). The accuracy is flat, about 97%, from 4 to 14 components. So the choice of \(M\) does not need to be exact: anything in that range works. Compare this with the rules of thumb in Part 3 (5 to 10 components). Here the outcome gives a sharper reason to stop than the variance alone.

Step 3: Fit and test

The model with \(M = 8\) components classifies about 98% of the held-out tumors correctly (3 mistakes in 142). The model with all 30 components (which uses the same information as the 30-feature logistic regression) does worse, at about 94%. The test set has only 142 tumors, so one tumor is 0.7 percentage points. Do not read much into differences of one or two tumors between neighbouring values of \(M\). The cross-validation curve, which uses all 427 training tumors, is more reliable.

Back to the original features

The price of PCR is interpretability: the coefficients \(\theta_m\) belong to components. But the model is still linear in the features. Since \(Z_m = \sum_j \phi_{jm} X_j\), the coefficient on the original (standardized) feature \(j\) is \[\beta_j = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \theta_m \, \phi_{jm}.\] The slides call this constraining the coefficients: the 30 values of \(\beta_j\) are not free, because they are built from only \(M\) numbers.

The two ways of computing the linear predictor agree. Notice that the weight is spread over groups of related features (several size features, several concavity features), because the components give related features similar loadings. The lasso in Lab 6 behaves differently: it tends to keep one feature from each correlated group and drop the rest. Neither is “right”. PCR is a good choice when you want to use all the information in a group of correlated measurements. The lasso is a good choice when you want a short list of features to measure.

Two cautions about PCR.

  • It chooses the components without looking at the outcome. It worked here because PC1 separates the classes (Part 5). If the signal were in a low-variance component, PCR with a small \(M\) would miss it.
  • It is not a feature-selection method. Every component uses all 30 features, so you still need to measure all 30 to score a new patient.

Things worth remembering

  • PCA finds new axes that capture variance. Each component is a weighted sum of the features. The first has the most variance, and each later one has the most variance among those uncorrelated with the earlier ones.
  • Scale first. PCA follows variance, and variance depends on units. On raw data, one feature with a big unit took over the first component.
  • PCA is an eigendecomposition. The loadings are eigenvectors of the correlation matrix, and the variances of the scores are its eigenvalues. With two standardized features, PC1 explains \((1 + \rho)/2\).
  • There is no single right \(M\) in PCA. The elbow, 80% cumulative PVE and variance above 1 gave 3 to 4, 5, and 6 components. Use the rule to get a range, not an answer.
  • Variance and compression are one idea. The error of rebuilding the data from \(M\) components is \(1 -\) the cumulative PVE.
  • PCR is regression on the first \(M\) scores. The scores are uncorrelated, so the instability from correlated features goes away. \(M\) is chosen by cross-validation. The coefficients can be mapped back to the features by \(\beta_j = \sum_m \theta_m \phi_{jm}\).
  • PCR does not see the outcome when it builds the components. It works when the high-variance directions are the useful ones. Check this before you trust it.
  • Components are not features you can measure on their own. A PCR model still needs all \(p\) original measurements for each new patient, and the names we give to components are our reading, not something in the data.
