Introduction

In Lab 7, k-means put every thyroid patient in exactly one cluster. At the end we said that a patient on the border between two groups needs a different tool. This lab uses that tool, the Gaussian mixture model (GMM), on a classic clinical question:

Can glucose and insulin tests, with no diagnosis given, separate normal patients from patients with early and with full diabetes? And can the method tell us which patients are hard to place?

We know the diagnosis for these patients, so we can check the answer. In real unsupervised work there is no answer key. The lab shows what to look at then.

The plan:

  1. Look at the data. Three tests, and clusters that are long and thin.
  2. k-means first. Where it goes wrong, and why.
  3. EM by hand. We write the E-step and the M-step from the slides, and watch the fit improve.
  4. Read the fitted model. Weights, means, and covariances in clinical units.
  5. Soft assignment. The probability that each patient belongs to each cluster, and who is uncertain.
  6. Choosing \(K\). BIC against the silhouette.
  7. Choosing the covariance shape. Full, diagonal, or spherical.
  8. Starting points. EM can stop at a bad answer.

A note on the tools

Two choices in this lab differ from earlier labs.

  • caret does the preprocessing. As in Lab 7, clustering has no outcome, so train() does not apply. We use preProcess() to center and scale.
  • We write EM ourselves. The mclust package fits GMMs in one line, but it is not available in the browser. So we code the algorithm from the slides, using dmvnorm() from the mvtnorm package for the normal density. It is about 30 lines. We checked the code against mclust on these data: the log-likelihood and BIC agree.

The data: glucose tolerance in 145 adults

Reaven and Miller (1979) studied 145 non-obese adults. Each person had three measurements:

Column Test What it measures
glucose glucose response area under the plasma glucose curve during a 3-hour oral glucose tolerance test
insulin insulin response area under the plasma insulin curve during the same test
sspg steady-state plasma glucose how well the body responds to insulin. A high value means insulin resistance

Doctors also gave each person a diagnosis: Normal, Chemical diabetes (an abnormal glucose test but no symptoms yet), or Overt diabetes (clinical diabetes). We hide the diagnosis from the algorithm and use it only to check.

What a clinician expects

The median of each test, by diagnosis:

Overt patients have the highest glucose and insulin responses, by a wide margin. Chemical patients have a glucose response close to normal, but their sspg is the highest. Their cells respond poorly to insulin. This is the early stage of the disease. Normal patients are low on all three tests.

The shape of the groups

Three things are visible:

  • The scales differ. The SD of insulin is 320. The SD of glucose is 64. We scale before clustering, as in Lab 7.
  • The groups are not round. The overt patients lie along a long, thin band. The normal patients form a small blob with a thin tail. The groups have different sizes and different orientations.
  • The chemical and normal groups overlap in glucose, and the chemical and overt groups overlap in insulin.

A clinician would say: some patients are borderline. We will check whether the algorithm finds them.

Part 1 — k-means first

Lab 7 gave us k-means, so we start there. We use \(K = 3\) and many starts. To compare cluster numbers with diagnoses, we give the clusters a fixed order: from the lowest to the highest mean glucose. This puts “Normal, Chemical, Overt” in the same order as the diagnoses.

About three patients in four are in the “right” cluster. The mistakes are large. Only 13 of the 36 chemical patients land in their own cluster, and 23 go into the normal cluster. Also, 9 of the 33 overt patients end up in the normal cluster. Here is the picture:

Why does k-means fail? k-means assigns each patient to the nearest centroid by Euclidean distance. The boundary between two clusters is a straight line halfway between their centroids. So k-means implicitly assumes that every cluster is a round ball of the same size. These clusters are long and thin, and they have different sizes. A long thin cluster is cut in two, or it takes patients from its neighbours.

A GMM fixes this by giving each cluster its own mean, its own covariance matrix (size, shape, and orientation), and its own weight.

Part 2 — EM by hand

The slides define the model: each cluster \(k\) has a weight \(\pi_k\), a mean \(\mu_k\), and a covariance \(\Sigma_k\). The density of a patient is \[p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, N(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k).\] We do not know the cluster of any patient, so we use EM. It alternates two steps.

TipDo this on paper first

In class we derived the E-step and the M-step for two clusters. Before you read the code, write them out for three clusters (\(K = 3\)):

  1. Write the model: the three weights \(\pi_1, \pi_2, \pi_3\) (what must they add up to?), the three means, and the three covariances.
  2. Write the E-step for \(r_{n1}\), \(r_{n2}\) and \(r_{n3}\). How many terms are in the denominator now? What must \(r_{n1} + r_{n2} + r_{n3}\) equal?
  3. Write the M-step for \(N_k\), \(\hat\mu_k\), \(\hat\Sigma_k\) and \(\hat\pi_k\). Check that \(N_1 + N_2 + N_3 = N\).
  4. Which lines changed from the two-cluster case? (Only the number of terms changes. The formulas keep the same form for any \(K\).)

Then compare your answer with the formulas and the code below.

E-step. Given the parameters, compute for each patient \(n\) and cluster \(k\) the probability that the patient belongs to the cluster (the responsibility): \[r_{nk} = \hat P(Y = k \mid x_n) = \frac{\pi_k \, N(x_n \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_j \pi_j \, N(x_n \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}.\]

M-step. Given the responsibilities, update the parameters. Each patient counts in cluster \(k\) with weight \(r_{nk}\): \[N_k = \sum_n r_{nk}, \quad \hat\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n r_{nk}\, x_n, \quad \hat\Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n r_{nk}\,(x_n - \hat\mu_k)(x_n - \hat\mu_k)^\top, \quad \hat\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{N}.\]

The formulas hold for any \(K\), and the code follows them line by line. The comments in the code show what happens when \(K = 3\).

Now the loop. EM needs a starting point. We start from the k-means clusters: we treat them as hard responsibilities (0 or 1), run one M-step to get starting parameters, and then alternate E and M until the log-likelihood stops changing. At the end we number the clusters by increasing glucose, as before.

EM stopped after a few dozen iterations. The log-likelihood is the quantity EM increases at every step, never decreases:

It rises fast at first, then slowly. EM is guaranteed to climb, but it climbs to a local maximum. Part 7 returns to this.

Watching the clusters move

We stop EM after 1, 3, 10 and 45 iterations, and draw each cluster as an ellipse (the 95% region of its normal distribution). Iteration 1 is the k-means start, with its hard assignments.

Iteration 1 is the fit to the k-means labels. Over the next iterations, the big ellipse (overt) widens to cover the whole band, including the patients that k-means gave away. The two small ellipses (normal and chemical) overlap, and they keep overlapping at the end, because the two groups do overlap in the data. A hard-assignment method cannot represent that overlap. A GMM can.

Part 3 — Reading the fitted model

First, does the GMM recover the diagnoses?

Compare with k-means in Part 1. The share of patients in the “right” cluster rises from 75% to 86%. 26 of the 36 chemical patients are in their own cluster, up from 13. No overt patient is lost to the normal cluster any more.

The cluster descriptions

A GMM gives three deliverables (from the slides): labels, probabilities, and descriptions of each cluster. The weights \(\hat\pi_k\) say how common each cluster is. The means \(\hat\mu_k\) say where it sits. We map the means back to clinical units:

The covariance matrices tell us how the tests move together inside each cluster. The correlations:

Inside a cluster, the tests are related, and the relations differ by cluster. In the normal cluster, the tests are nearly unrelated (correlations 0.22 or less). In the overt cluster, glucose and insulin responses move together (correlation 0.95), and both fall as sspg rises (about -0.7). A model that forces the correlations to zero, or the clusters to be round, misses this. Part 6 tests that.

Part 4 — Soft assignment: who is hard to place?

So far we used only the most probable cluster. The full output is the matrix of responsibilities \(r_{nk}\), one row per patient. The highest probability in a row measures how sure the model is. Here are patient 1 and the three patients the model is least sure about:

Each row sums to 1. Patient 1 has a row like (0.99, 0.01, 0.00): clearly in one cluster. The other three have probability split between two clusters: they sit on a border.

Over all patients:

Most patients have a probability near 1. A group of 23 patients, left of the red line, are uncertain. Which diagnosis do they have? 12 of the 23 are chemical diabetes, the intermediate stage. That is one third of all chemical patients. This is the clinical story: chemical diabetes sits between normal and overt, and the model says so by splitting the probability of those patients across clusters.

k-means has no such output. It assigns every patient with full confidence, including the ones that a clinician would call borderline. In practice, we can use this: send patients below a probability threshold for a second look, and treat the confident ones as settled.

A warning. The probabilities come from the model. They are only as good as the assumption that each cluster is a normal distribution. A model with a wrong shape gives confident but wrong probabilities.

Part 5 — Choosing \(K\)

So far we used \(K = 3\) because we know there are three diagnoses. In real work we do not. The slides give two tools: BIC (parametric) and the silhouette (non-parametric).

BIC rewards fit and penalizes parameters: \[\text{BIC} = 2\,\ell(\hat\theta) - d \log N,\] where \(\ell\) is the log-likelihood and \(d\) is the number of free parameters. A full-covariance GMM with \(K\) clusters in \(p\) dimensions has \(d = K\left(p + \tfrac{p(p+1)}{2}\right) + (K - 1)\): a mean, a covariance, and a weight per cluster (the weights sum to 1). Higher BIC is better.

The log-likelihood always rises with \(K\). BIC peaks at \(K = 3\) and then falls, because extra clusters cost more in parameters than they gain in fit.

The silhouette needs hard labels. We use the GMM’s most probable cluster, and for comparison k-means labels:

BIC says \(K = 3\). The silhouette says \(K = 2\), for both methods, and gives \(K = 3\) a clearly lower value. Why?

The silhouette measures compact, well-separated blobs. The cleanest split of these data is “overt” against “everyone else”, because the overt group is far away. The chemical and normal groups overlap, so splitting them lowers the silhouette. BIC asks a different question: does a three-cluster model explain the data better, given the cost of its parameters? Overlapping groups with different shapes are exactly what a mixture model can represent.

Neither tool is always right. When they disagree, look at the clusters, and ask someone who knows the domain. Here a clinician would not accept “overt against everyone else”, because chemical diabetes is a known, separate stage.

Part 6 — Choosing the covariance shape

A GMM has a second choice that k-means does not have: the shape of each cluster’s covariance. The code from Part 2 supports three:

cov_type Each cluster is Free covariance parameters per cluster
"spherical" a round ball, with its own size 1
"diag" an ellipse aligned with the axes (tests uncorrelated) 3
"full" any ellipse, any tilt 6

k-means is close to the first row, with all clusters the same size, and hard assignments. We fit all three for \(K = 1, \dots, 6\):

The full-covariance model has the best BIC by a wide margin, at \(K = 3\) (-483, against -559 for diagonal and -759 for spherical). Look at the other two curves:

  • Diagonal peaks later, around \(K = 4\).
  • Spherical is still rising at \(K = 6\). A round ball is the wrong shape for a long thin cluster, so the model uses several balls to cover one band.

The wrong covariance shape inflates the number of clusters. This is the GMM version of what happened to k-means in Part 1.

The three shapes also give different clusterings at \(K = 3\):

Round clusters do about as badly as k-means (79% against 75%). Allowing each cluster to stretch, with or without a tilt, gives 86%. The diagonal and full models agree equally well with the diagnoses at \(K = 3\). BIC still prefers the full model. The three extra parameters per cluster (the correlations) buy a much better fit, and the diagonal model needs a fourth cluster to make up for the tilt it cannot represent.

Part 7 — Starting points

EM climbs to a local maximum of the log-likelihood, so the start matters, just as for k-means in Lab 7. We started from k-means, which is a good start. How bad is a random one? We run EM 30 times from random cluster labels:

Only about one run in five reaches the best solution. The others stop at worse local maxima, each with a different set of clusters, and nothing in the output warns you. So:

  • Start from several different initial clusterings, and keep the fit with the highest log-likelihood.
  • Initializing from k-means is a common and good default.
  • Set a seed so that you and your readers get the same clusters.

Try it yourself

1. A responsibility by hand. For the least certain patient (i), compute \(r_{nk}\) directly from the formula, using dmvnorm(), and check that it equals that patient’s row of gmm3$r.

2. Four clusters. Fit \(K = 4\) with full covariances (fits[[4]]). Cross the clusters with the diagnoses. Which diagnosis is split in two? Profile the clusters in clinical units (copy the code from Part 3). Does the new cluster make clinical sense?

3. A different threshold. In Part 4, how many patients have a highest probability below 0.7? Below 0.99? What is the diagnosis mix at each threshold? What would be a sensible rule for sending patients for a second look?

4. Fewer tests. A clinic may measure only glucose and insulin. Fit a GMM to z[, 1:2] with \(K = 3\) (the E-step and M-step work for any number of columns, but bic() uses p, so skip BIC). How do the clusters compare with the three-test fit?

Things worth remembering

  • A GMM models the clusters; k-means only partitions the points. Each cluster has a weight, a mean, and its own covariance matrix.
  • Soft assignment is the main gain. The probability of belonging to each cluster shows who is borderline. Here the borderline patients were mostly the chemical, intermediate stage.
  • EM alternates two easy steps. The E-step computes responsibilities. The M-step re-estimates parameters with those responsibilities as weights. The log-likelihood never goes down.
  • EM finds local maxima. Use several starts and keep the best.
  • BIC and the silhouette answer different questions. BIC asked whether a model fits well for its size. The silhouette asked whether the blobs are compact. They disagreed here, and a domain expert settles it.
  • The covariance shape is a modelling choice. Too restrictive a shape (round, or axis-aligned) needs extra clusters to cover the data. Compare shapes by BIC.
  • The model is an assumption. The probabilities are only as good as the normal-cluster assumption.
