Course description

This 2-credit course introduces basic concepts and methods for statistical learning, with an emphasis on modern health science applications. Topics include linear regression with regularization, supervised classification, unsupervised clustering, and dimensionality reduction. The target audience is second-year Biostatistics master’s students or early PhD students with interests in statistical learning techniques for health science data. Students will be trained with hands-on materials to understand the methods, implement the algorithms, and interpret results in real applications.

Prerequisites

BIOST 2141, 2142, and 3011. Students are expected to have programming experience in R, or in a low-level language such as C, C++, Java, or Fortran. Prerequisites can be met with other relevant coursework or experience with consent from the instructor.

Learning objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

  1. Explain the motivation and intuition behind the statistical learning methods covered in class.
  2. Demonstrate basic theory proofs for selected fundamental concepts and methods.
  3. Implement methods on real datasets in health sciences.
  4. Produce an analysis pipeline and interpret the results in a real application.

Textbook

Primary textbook (highly recommended; free PDF):

  • James, Witten, Hastie & Tibshirani. An Introduction to Statistical Learning: with Applications in R. Springer, 2013. https://www.statlearning.com/

Optional references:

  • Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman. The Elements of Statistical Learning. Springer, 2009.
  • Prince. Understanding Deep Learning. MIT Press, 2024.
  • Bishop. Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning. Springer, 2006.

Quiz policy

A short 5-question quiz follows each of the 12 lecture topics, released on Canvas the same day as lecture and due before the next lecture. Quizzes check basic comprehension of that week’s material and are graded on correctness. Your lowest two quiz scores are dropped before averaging, so a couple of missed or rough weeks don’t disproportionately affect your grade. No late quizzes — the drop policy exists instead of a late-submission exception.

Homework policy

Five homework assignments, each covering two consecutive lecture topics (modules 2–11) — a mix of theory, simulation, and applied data analysis in R. Each is scoped to about 5 hours of work, released once both of its covered lectures have been taught and due two weeks later. Multiple hypothesis testing (module 12) has no homework, only its quiz. Submissions are electronic via Canvas. Discussion of homework with classmates is allowed, but each student must independently write and implement their own solution. Solutions are graded on both correctness and clarity. Partial credit is given for identifying gaps in an argument when a complete solution cannot be derived.

Late homework: 80% credit if 0–1 days late, 50% credit if 1–2 days, 0% if more than 2 days. Exceptions for sickness or emergency require the instructor be informed in advance.

Final project

By 11/06, students submit a one-page proposal identifying either a dataset to analyze or a methodological paper to review. On 12/04, each student gives a 5-minute presentation summarizing their work. See the project page for details.
